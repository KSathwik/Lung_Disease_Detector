# Literature notes (related-work synthesis)

Citations use keys in `references.bib`. Claims rest on the cited paper's abstract or title only, as recorded in `REFERENCES_STATUS.md`. Keys marked (title only) support only what the title says. No number from any cited paper is compared with this project's numbers.

## (a) Public chest X-ray datasets and their label quality

Large public collections underpin most chest X-ray (CXR) deep learning. ChestX-ray8, later ChestX-ray14, provided hospital-scale images with weakly supervised labels for common thoracic findings [wang2017chestxray8]. CheXpert added uncertainty labels and an expert comparison [irvin2019chexpert]. MIMIC-CXR paired radiographs with free-text reports [johnson2019mimic], and VinDr-CXR offered radiologist annotations [nguyen2022vindr]. The CheXNet study applied a DenseNet-style network to pneumonia detection on this kind of data [rajpurkar2017chexnet]. For tuberculosis (TB), two small sets (Montgomery and Shenzhen) were released together [jaeger2014two], with lung segmentations for the former [candemir2014lung]. TBX11K was later introduced as a larger TB dataset [liu2020rethinking]. A paediatric pneumonia set accompanied a widely cited transfer-learning study [kermany2018identifying], and the JSRT database supplies nodule and non-nodule radiographs [shiraishi2000development]. COVID-19 images were first gathered as a community collection from published sources [cohen2020covid], and a curated COVID-19 radiography database followed [chowdhury2020can].

These resources differ in how labels were produced. Oakden-Rayner reviewed about 700 images from ChestX-ray14 with a radiologist and found that its labels did not accurately reflect image content: positive predictive values were mostly 10% to 30% lower than the original documentation reported, and he also described hidden stratification and label disambiguation failures [oakdenrayner2020exploring]. Hidden stratification means that an aggregate label hides clinically distinct subsets on which the model may fail [oakdenrayner2020hidden]. The consequence for multi-source work is direct: when a six-class benchmark is assembled from datasets that were labelled in different ways, the class label is partly a record of where the image came from and how it was labelled, not only of the disease. The COVID-19 collection itself carries this caveat in its title, which argues that prospective predictions, not retrospective ones on pooled public images, are the future [cohen2020covid].

## (b) Multi-class CXR classifiers, including COVID-19 and TB

Early COVID-19 work reported transfer learning from ImageNet-pretrained networks [deng2009imagenet] for COVID-19 detection [apostolopoulos2020covid]. COVID-Net proposed a tailored architecture for the same task [wang2020covidnet]. DarkCovidNet addressed COVID-19, no-finding and pneumonia classes [ozturk2020automated]. Pruned CNN ensembles were applied to COVID-19 detection in CXR [rajaraman2020iteratively]. The COVID-QU/radiography line of work from Chowdhury and colleagues used viral pneumonia and normal controls [chowdhury2020can], and the same group studied the effect of image enhancement on COVID-19 detection [rahman2021exploring]. For TB, the same group combined deep learning, lung segmentation and visualisation [rahman2020reliable]. Segmentation-plus-classification approaches for TB continue to appear in Biomedical Signal Processing and Control (BSPC) [iqbal2023tuberculosis].

Three-condition or multi-condition formulations are common in BSPC. Amin et al. address COVID-19, TB and pneumonia with an ensemble [amin2024automated] (title only). Nayak et al. compare deep CNNs for COVID-19 detection [nayak2021application] (title only). Bassi and Attux classify COVID-19, normal and pneumonia with a DenseNet201 behind a U-Net lung-segmentation stage, and report that accuracy on an external dataset was lower than internal accuracy, which they read as difficulty in generalising [bassi2022covid]. DenseNet itself is the backbone in many of these studies [huang2017densely], and its use for CXR classification dates to CheXNet [rajpurkar2017chexnet].

Two limits of this literature matter here. First, the classes are usually few and clinically dissimilar (COVID-19, normal, viral or bacterial pneumonia); six-class settings that include pleural effusion and pulmonary nodules or masses are rarer. I did not find a verified six-class comparison on a common protocol, so none is claimed. Second, the headline numbers come from the evaluation designs discussed next, so they cannot be compared across papers.

## (c) Shortcut learning, dataset or source confounding, and leakage

Geirhos et al. define shortcut learning: networks adopt decision rules that work on the benchmark but fail under shifted conditions [geirhos2020shortcut]. In CXR this has been shown repeatedly.

- Zech et al. trained pneumonia models across three hospital systems. Prevalence differed greatly between sites (34.2% at one, about 1% at the other two), so that merely sorting images by hospital system gave an AUC of 0.861 on the joint data of two sites. Performance varied across external sites [zech2018variable]. A model can therefore score well by recognising the source.
- DeGrave et al. applied explainable-AI methods to COVID-19 CXR detectors. They report that the systems relied on confounding factors, not medical pathology, and appeared accurate but failed when tested in new hospitals [degrave2021ai] (wording taken from the preprint abstract, see status file).
- Maguolo and Nanni trained classifiers on images with the central lung region turned black and obtained similar results. They conclude that several testing protocols are not fair and that networks learn dataset patterns uncorrelated with COVID-19 [maguolo2020critic]. This is evidence that high accuracy on a random image-level split of pooled public data can come from the source, not the disease.
- Tartaglione et al. show how much bias can enter when a network is pre-trained on larger non-COVID public CXR datasets and then fine-tuned on small COVID data, and they validate on a new hospital dataset [tartaglione2020unveiling].
- A BSPC study analyses the Clever Hans effect in COVID-19 detection on CXR with Bayesian deep learning [arias2024analysis] (title only).
- Patient race can be recognised from medical images by deep models [gichoya2022ai] (title only), and under-diagnosis differs across under-served patient groups [seyyedkalantari2021underdiagnosis] (title only). Both indicate that images carry attributes unrelated to the label.

Systematic reviews describe the consequence at field level. Roberts et al. screened 2,212 COVID-19 CXR/CT machine-learning studies, kept 62 after quality screening, and concluded that none of the models was of potential clinical use because of methodological flaws or underlying biases [roberts2021common]. Wynants et al. appraised 232 COVID-19 prediction models (75 imaging-based diagnostic ones among them) with PROBAST for risk of bias [wynants2020prediction]. Kapoor and Narayanan surveyed 17 fields where leakage was found, affecting 294 papers and sometimes giving wildly over-optimistic conclusions, and propose a taxonomy of eight leakage types [kapoor2023leakage]. For this project the relevant leakage types are patient-level overlap between splits and duplicate images, which is why splits are grouped by patient where identity is known.

Where the literature's accuracies come from leaky or single-source evaluations: the cited papers themselves support this only in specific cases. Maguolo and Nanni show it for the COVID-19 testing protocols they examine [maguolo2020critic]. DeGrave et al. show it for the COVID-19 detectors they analysed [degrave2021ai]. Zech et al. show source recognition inflating pooled-data performance in their setup [zech2018variable]. Roberts et al. attribute the unsuitability of the 62 reviewed models to methodological flaws and biases, without attributing a specific accuracy value to leakage [roberts2021common]. I make no claim about any individual classifier in section (b) beyond what these papers say.

## (d) External validation and generalisation

Yu et al. reviewed 83 studies (86 algorithms) of radiology deep learning with external validation: 81% reported at least some drop in external performance, 49% a drop of at least 0.05 on the unit scale, and 24% a drop of at least 0.10 [yu2022external]. Zech et al. found variable cross-hospital generalisation for pneumonia [zech2018variable]. Domain shift in CXR classification [pooch2020can] (title only) and the limits of cross-domain generalisation in automated X-ray prediction [cohen2020limits] (title only) address the same issue. Bassi and Attux report an external-versus-internal accuracy gap for a COVID/normal/pneumonia model [bassi2022covid]. Hidden stratification shows that even a good aggregate external figure can hide poor subsets [oakdenrayner2020hidden].

A fair external check needs a cohort that was not used for fitting, selection or threshold tuning, and it must report sensitivity together with specificity, because a cohort made only of positives, or a model that over-predicts one class, can hide failure in either direction. This project uses the Montgomery TB set [jaeger2014two] as the external cohort and reports sensitivity with specificity; the numbers are produced from the final model and are not repeated here.

## (e) Explainability and calibration

Grad-CAM produces coarse class-discriminative heatmaps from gradients at a convolutional layer [selvaraju2017gradcam]. It is attractive for CXR demonstrations, but its fidelity is contested. Arun et al. tested eight saliency techniques on pneumothorax and pneumonia CXR data: all failed at least one of the localisation, weight-randomisation sensitivity, repeatability and reproducibility criteria, and they were inferior to dedicated localisation networks [arun2021assessing]. Ghassemi et al. argue that current explainable-AI methods give no reliable assurance for individual decisions in health care and should not substitute for rigorous validation [ghassemi2021false]. DeGrave et al. used attribution methods to expose shortcuts, which shows the use of saliency as an audit tool, not as proof of correct reasoning [degrave2021ai]. In this project Grad-CAM is therefore presented as an illustrative aid, not as localisation evidence.

Calibration is a separate question from accuracy. Guo et al. show that modern deep networks tend to be miscalibrated and evaluate temperature scaling [guo2017calibration]. The binned expected calibration error follows the binning idea of Bayesian binning methods [naeini2015obtaining]. Confidence intervals for proportions use the Wilson interval [wilson1927probable], and resampling-based intervals follow the bootstrap [efron1994introduction]. Reliability diagrams on a held-out test set are reported to show whether displayed probabilities can be read as probabilities.

## (f) Reporting guidelines

CLAIM is a checklist for authors and reviewers of AI studies in medical imaging [mongan2020claim]. TRIPOD+AI updates the TRIPOD statement for prediction models that use regression or machine learning [collins2024tripodai]. This project's manuscripts follow both as structure for the methods and limitations sections: data sources and selection, labelling, split design, evaluation set independence, performance with uncertainty, and limitations. The systematic reviews cited above point to the same weaknesses these guidelines target [roberts2021common; wynants2020prediction].

## Research gap addressed by this project

The verified literature shows that (i) public CXR labels and sources are heterogeneous [oakdenrayner2020exploring; wang2017chestxray8; jaeger2014two], (ii) classifiers on pooled public data can exploit source and dataset artefacts [maguolo2020critic; degrave2021ai; zech2018variable], (iii) reviews rate most COVID-19 imaging models as unfit for clinical use because of method flaws [roberts2021common; wynants2020prediction], (iv) external performance usually falls below internal performance [yu2022external], and (v) saliency maps are weak evidence [arun2021assessing; ghassemi2021false]. What is less available are fully documented, auditable multi-source six-class CXR pipelines (COVID-19, normal, pleural effusion, pneumonia, nodule or mass, TB) that are leakage-controlled and rebuilt from official sources, and that report what happens when an earlier pipeline's leakage is removed. This project contributes: an auditable, leakage-controlled rebuild of a multi-source six-class benchmark from official sources, with patient-grouped splits where identity is known (and per-image grouping stated where it is not); a reproducible end-to-end pipeline from acquisition to served metrics with once-only test and external evaluation; evidence that internal metrics overstate generalisation, from an external cohort and a source shortcut analysis; and an audit of how an earlier pipeline's patient leakage and proxy labels inflated its reported accuracy. It is a single seed-42 CPU run per model and makes no claim of clinical validity.
